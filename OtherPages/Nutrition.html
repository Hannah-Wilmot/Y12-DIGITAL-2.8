Teen Nutrition Guide: Eat well to feel well


Why Nutition Matters


What you eat affects how you feel, grow, and function every day. A healthy diet improves your concentration, energy, and mood. Over time, it can prevent chronic conditions like diabetes, heart disease, and some cancers. Even one extra serving of vegetables a day can have a big impact—both for you and the New Zealands’s health system.



Eat Regularly and Mindfully


Eating at regular times helps balance your energy levels and keeps blood sugar stable, which is especially important for rangatahi at risk of diabetes. Don’t skip meals, especially breakfast. Eating with others and helping prepare meals builds skills, creates connection, and supports positive food habits.


  • Aim for three balanced meals and two to three healthy snacks each day.
  • Don’t skip breakfast—it jumpstarts energy and brain function.
  • Share meals with whānau or friends when possible and avoid devices during meals.
  • Involve rangatahi in cooking and meal prep to build practical life skills and food confidence.


Balance the Four Core Food Groups Daily


Your body needs a mix of nutrients each day. The four main food groups—vegetables and fruit, grains, dairy or alternatives, and protein foods—each play a role in growth, energy, and wellbeing. Eating a variety across all groups ensures you get what your body needs.



1. Vegetables and Fruit


Vegetables and fruits are nutritional powerhouses. They provide fibre for digestion, vitamins for immunity, and antioxidants that protect your body. Try to eat a rainbow of colours and make them part of every meal or snack. Prepping them in advance helps make healthy choices easy.


  • Recommended: At least five servings of vegetables and two servings of fruit each day.
  • Vegetables and fruits provide fibre, vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants.
  • Each extra serve of vegetables is associated with a reduced risk of chronic disease.
  • Make veg easy to access—keep them prepped in the fridge, or add them to stir-fries, smoothies, and sandwiches.
  • Examples of one serving:
    1. ½ cup cooked vegetables
    2. 1 cup raw salad
    3. 1 medium sized fruit


2. Grains and Starchy Foods


Wholegrains like brown rice, oats, and wholemeal bread are full of energy, fibre, and nutrients that help your brain and body work well. They also help you feel full for longer. Choose whole grain options most often and aim for at least seven servings a day.


  • Recommended: At least seven servings daily (more if very active).
  • Choose whole grain options like brown rice, oats, and wholemeal bread to boost fibre intake.
  • Examples of one serving:
    1. ½ cup cooked pasta or rice
    2. 1 slice of bread
    3. ½ cup porridge or oats


3. Milk and Dairy (or Fortified Alternatives)

  • Examples of one serving:
    1. 250 ml milk
    2. 1 small yoghurt tub
    3. 40 g cheese


    • 4. Protein-Rich Foods


    Protein helps build muscle, strengthen bones, and support your immune system. It also provides iron, zinc, and other essential nutrients. Whether you eat meat or plant-based foods, aim for at least two servings of protein each day and include a variety.


    • Recommended: At least two servings daily.
    • Protein helps build and repair muscles, supports brain and immune function, and delivers essential minerals like iron and zinc.
    • Examples of one serving:
      1. 2 eggs
      2. 100 g cooked lean meat or fish
      3. ½ cup legumes (chickpeas, beans, lentils)
      4. ⅓ cup nuts or seeds


    For Vegetarian or Vegan Rangatahi


    You can meet your nutrition needs without meat—but it takes some planning. Focus on plant-based iron sources like lentils, beans, wholegrains, and leafy greens. Pair them with foods high in vitamin C (like citrus fruit or capsicum) to help your body absorb the iron. Choose fortified plant milks and consider supplements if needed.


    • Include iron-rich foods: lentils, tofu, wholegrains, and leafy greens.
    • Eat vitamin C-rich foods (like oranges, capsicum, or kiwi) to help absorb iron.
    • Use fortified plant milks for calcium, iodine, and vitamin B12.
    • nclude nuts, seeds, fortified foods, and moderate sun exposure to meet omega-3 and vitamin D needs.


    Smart Snacking and Staying Hydrated


    Snacks help fuel you between meals—but not all snacks are equal. Reach for options that give lasting energy, like fruit, nuts, yoghurt, or wholegrain crackers. Water should be your main drink. Avoid sugary drinks and limit dried fruit and juice, which are high in natural sugars.


    • Water should be the main drink—it's best for hydration and overall health.
    • Healthy snacks include fresh fruit, unsweetened yoghurt, vegetables with hummus, wholegrain crackers, and a small handful of nuts
    • Limit sugary drinks such as fizzy drinks and juice.
    • Dried fruit is high in sugar—keep it to a small serving per day.


    Making Healthier Takeaway Choices


    Takeaways are fine in moderation, especially when you make smarter picks. Choose grilled, fresh, or veggie-packed options and go easy on cheese, mayo, and large servings. Add a salad or fruit to balance the meal. Planning ahead can help avoid last-minute less healthy choices.


    • Choose grilled wraps, salads, sandwiches, or sushi over deep-fried options.
    • Go easy on extras like cheese, creamy sauces, and large portions.
    • Add a side of vegetables or fruit where possible to boost nutritional value.


    Practical Tips for Everyday Eating


    Eating well doesn’t have to cost a lot or take heaps of time. Use seasonal produce, shop local, and don’t be afraid of frozen veg—they’re just as nutritious. Prep meals in batches and involve the whānau. Small changes—like adding one extra veg—really do add up over time.


    • Use seasonal and budget-friendly produce—shop at local markets or look for frozen veg specials.
    • Growing your own fruits or vegetables is a great way to eat fresh and save money.
    • Prepare meals in advance to save time and avoid relying on less healthy convenience foods.
    • Focus on small, manageable changes like adding one extra serving of vegetables or cooking one more meal at home per week.


    What is a Serving?


    A serving is a standard way to measure how much of a particular food counts toward your daily nutrition goals. Serving sizes help you track your intake of different food groups like vegetables, fruit, grains, protein, and dairy.

    Key Takeaways


    • Eat regularly: three meals and healthy snacks each day.
    • Include all four core food groups daily.
    • Drink water, not sugary drinks.
    • Choose whole and minimally processed foods whenever possible.
    • Start small—little changes over time lead to lifelong habits.
