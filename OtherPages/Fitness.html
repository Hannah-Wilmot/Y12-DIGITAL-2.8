Teen Fitness Guide: Move Well, Stay Strong


Why Fitness Matters for Teens

Regular physical activity supports your physical, mental, and emotional wellbeing — helping with growth, strength, focus, mood, and energy. It also builds habits that protect you from health issues like diabetes, obesity, and heart disease later in life. For rangatahi, fitness is about feeling good and strong in your body, whatever your starting point.



How Much Activity Do Teens Need?


Activity Type Recommendation
Physical activity (any movement) At least 60 minutes daily
Muscle-strengthening 3+ days per week
Bone-strengthening 3+ days per week (if appropriate)
Screen time (non-school) Less than 2 hours/day

Note: These are flexible goals. What matters is moving regularly and consistently in ways that feel safe and doable for you.



Types of Movement You Can Try

1. Aerobic (Heart-Pumping) Activity

Boosts heart and lung health.
Examples: brisk walking, wheeling, swimming, cycling, dancing, sports.


2. Strength-Based Activity

Improves muscles and body function.
Examples: bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, weight training, yoga.


3. Bone-Strengthening (Impact-Based) Activity

Important for growing strong bones.
Examples: jumping, skipping, sport drills, plyometrics — if mobility allows.



For Teens with Physical Disabilities

Everyone deserves to move in ways that feel empowering, regardless of ability level. If you have a disability, physical activity is still possible — and beneficial — with some adjustments:


  • Chair-based workouts: Include upper-body strength training resistance bands, or seated cardio.
  • Adaptive sports: Look into wheelchair basketball, swimming, para athletics, hand-cycling, or seated dance.
  • Range-of-motion and flexibility exercises: Stretching, yoga, or physiotherapy-based movement keeps your body mobile and reduces stiffness.
  • Water-based activity: Pool therapy or swimming can be lower impact and highly supportive.

Tips:

  • Focus on what you can do, not what you can’t.
  • Set personal goals based on your own ability and interests.
  • Work with your physio, OT, or whānau to build a safe, motivating routine.
  • Celebrate progress — even small improvements matter.


Weekly Movement Example

Day Activity Idea Focus
Monday Brisk walk, wheelchair rolling, or cycling Cardio
Tuesday Chair yoga or resistance bands Strength/Flex
Wednesday Swimming or pool exercises Low-impact Cardio
Thursday Strength training (bodyweight, bands, light weights) Muscle
Friday Sports training or dance Cardio/Coordination
Saturday Active outing (walk, adventure, nature) General movement
Sunday Stretching, breathing exercises, light mobility Recovery


Flexibility and Recovery

  • Stretch after exercise to keep muscles healthy.
  • Aim for at least one rest day each week.
  • Sleep 8–10 hours each night for repair, mental clarity, and mood regulation.
  • Listen to your body — rest if needed, and ask for help when unsure.


Fitness and Nutrition Work Together

Moving your body is more effective when you fuel it well:

  • Eat a mix of fruits, vegetables, grains, protein, and dairy or fortified alternatives.
  • Stay hydrated — water is best, especially before and after activity.
  • Avoid energy drinks and sugary soft drinks, which can dehydrate and reduce performance.


Staying Safe

  • Always warm up and cool down.
  • Learn correct form — use mirrors, videos, or a coach for guidance.
  • Start small. Increase intensity or duration gradually.
  • Wear supportive shoes and clothes for the activity you’re doing.
  • If you feel dizzy, unwell, or in pain, stop and rest.


Staying Motivated

  • Choose movement that feels fun: music, games, social sports, or outdoor time.
  • Exercise with friends, whānau, or as part of a team.
  • Use apps or videos tailored to your interests or ability.
  • Keep track of progress, not perfection.
  • Remember: every bit of movement helps — whether it's five minutes or fifty.

Common Challenges & How to Overcome Them

Challenge Tip
"I feel self-conscious" Try working out at home or with close friends first
"I don’t know what to do" Use beginner workout videos, talk to a teacher or trainer
"I don’t have equipment" Use bodyweight or household items (e.g., cans, towels)
"I have a disability" Modify exercises or find adaptive options with support
"I don’t like sport" Movement isn't just sport — try dancing, walking, or yoga


Final Word

Fitness is personal. You don’t have to be an athlete to benefit from moving your body. Whether you walk, wheel, swim, stretch, or dance — the key is to move in ways that feel right for you. Over time, you’ll build confidence, energy, and a strong foundation for your future.

Key Takeaways
